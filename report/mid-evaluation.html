A microgrid energy-mix optimiser for Banaskantha. Decides hour by hour how much load is served by solar, battery, the rationed agricultural feeder, the village feeder, or diesel — minimising cost while holding reliable supply. Modelled at three scales: one farm, a whole village, and a village whose neighbours share.

What the farm looks like today

baseline

A 5 HP diesel pumpset drives irrigation. It is a direct-coupled engine turning a pump shaft, so it cannot power anything else — household, dairy and cold-storage load sit on the village feeder with no backup at all. Gujarat’s Jyotigram scheme runs those two feeders separately and deliberately, and the split governs the whole problem: the agricultural feeder is three-phase and rationed to a published eight-hour block, while the village feeder is single-phase and near-continuous but too small to start the pump.

What we built

delivery status

The system is a deterministic forecasting-and-optimisation core with an applied layer on top. Every dispatch decision is made by a linear program, never by a language model.

The optimiser cannot flatter itself. The linear program only proposes actions; pymgrid then steps the microgrid and remains the authority on physics. An over-optimistic plan would surface as unserved load or overgeneration in the log. Both are zero across every reported run.
Two claims we do not make. There is no trained ML model anywhere — forecasting is statistical, calibrated against measured error. And the language model is confined to phrasing: it never computes a number, and generated text is rejected and replaced with deterministic English if any figure in it cannot be traced back to the solver’s own output.

Does optimisation beat rules?

result 1

Each controller was run over the same year, on the same hardware, against the same weather. The comparison that matters is the third column against the second — not against the status quo, because a good rule-based controller already captures most of the easy gain.

The optimiser removes 184 L of diesel and ₹18,867 a year beyond what rules achieve on identical hardware — 26% of the remaining bill. And 88% of that advantage survives once the controller plans on forecasts rather than perfect foresight.

Now read the CO2 row. Halving the diesel moved emissions by 4 kg across a whole year. The optimiser displaces diesel largely by importing more grid power, and once round-trip losses are counted the two emit about the same per kWh delivered. Cost and carbon are not the same objective, and optimising hard for one barely touches the other. What does move carbon is covered in result 6.

The optimiser changes what to buy

result 2 · central finding

Hardware was not assumed. Every combination of solar, wind and battery was simulated across the full year, costed with capital recovered over each asset’s life, and the cheapest configuration holding reliability was selected. Running that search under each controller gives different answers.

Cost-optimal sizing, by controller identical demand, prices and reliability floor · only the controller differs
ControllerSolarBatteryDiesel cut
Rule-based2 kWp0 kWh59.0%
Optimiser2 kWp5 kWh85.0%

Under simple rules a battery never earns back its cost, so the optimal system has none. Under optimisation the same battery pays for itself and nearly doubles the diesel displaced. The controller is not a layer on top of the hardware — it determines which hardware is worth installing. Tools that size first and dispatch second buy the wrong hardware.

Why. A myopic controller reacts to the present hour. The optimiser charges the battery during the subsidised agricultural window at ₹1.50/kWh and discharges it later against diesel at ₹73.79/kWh. Measured over the year, 94% of all battery charging occurs while that feeder is scheduled on.

Those two rows come from a sweep on synthetic weather; the real-weather recommendation is the 3 kWp / 5 kWh system. The comparison between controllers is the point, and it holds in both.

A village is not one farm multiplied

result 4

Two things change at village scale, and both matter.

Diversity. A hundred households do not switch on together, and twenty pumps do not start in the same minute. Each farm has its own fixed start hour, so the aggregate pump peak is 29.8 kW against the 74.6 kW they would draw simultaneously — a diversity factor of 0.40. That spread is the whole economic argument for shared infrastructure, and a model that multiplies one farm by twenty throws it away.

The peak moves to the evening. On one farm the dominant load is a pump running in daylight, so demand and solar roughly coincide. In a village, households dominate and peak at 31 kW at 19:00 when solar is zero, against a 10.9 kW trough at 13:00 when solar is strongest. That mismatch is precisely what storage exists to fix.

The routing constraint decides the answer

At farm scale, the rule that pumps cannot use the domestic feeder came for free: a 3 kW single-phase connection physically cannot start a 3.73 kW pump. A 50 kW village transformer can, so at village scale the rule has to be stated explicitly. It is a real constraint, not a modelling convenience — the two supplies are separately sanctioned and separately tariffed, and running a pump off a domestic connection is a different contract, not a clever optimisation.

Storage went from worthless to essential. Once pumps genuinely cannot fall back on the domestic feeder, the roughly 69% of hours when the agricultural feeder is down must be covered by solar, battery or diesel — and storage is the cheapest way. Every one of the ten cheapest configurations now carries a battery, the smallest being 25 kWh.

We report the lower number. The earlier ₹19,244 per household was inflated because the model was quietly supplying pumps from a domestic connection. The honest figure is 14% lower, and it is the one in the headline.
An independent consistency check. Status-quo diesel works out to 1,098 L per farm against 1,080 L from the separate single-farm run — within 1.7%, from a completely different load model and code path.

Who sharing actually helps

result 5

The village model above puts every load on one bus, which quietly assumes sharing is already perfect and free. You cannot price something you have assumed, so the village was re-modelled as its actual participants and run with only the line between them different. Energy crossing the line loses 3% and every connection is capacity-limited — without both, sharing would be free by construction and the result would mean nothing.

Cheapest and fairest are not the same line. 2 kW is cheapest per unit at ₹2.91 and still leaves 517 kWh unserved. 8 kW serves everybody for ₹3.19, about 9% more — and deliberately burns more diesel, 370 L against 154 L, because reaching the last household is worth more than the fuel it costs. Both rows are reported because that is a policy choice, not a technical one.

Running it as one village

With the line in place at 4 kW, the same cluster was run once with every flow attributed to its owner — 27 metered connections covering 122 premises, one battery fleet, one optimisation. It serves 99.73% of demand at ₹3.11/kWh with zero curtailment, on a supply mix of village feeder 39.2%, agricultural feeder 31.4%, own solar 28.6% and diesel 0.8%.

The farms are the only net exporters — they own every panel. But the households own no generation and still push out 470 kWh, so that number needed a mechanism rather than an assumption. Checking the hours: all 165 of them have the agricultural feeder off and the village feeder on, accounting for 100% of the 470 kWh. What crosses is village-feeder power relayed to the farms during the ration.

So this is a trade, not charity. Farm solar by day, household grid access during the ration — and the farms get something back. That is the difference between a scheme people join and one they have to be talked into. It falls out of the feeder rules rather than being designed in: households hold a domestic connection the farms cannot use for irrigation, and that entitlement turns out to be a tradable asset.

Banaskantha is inland northern Gujarat; the state’s wind resource sits on the Kutch and Saurashtra coast. A wind turbine here would be a decoration.

To confirm the engine follows the resource rather than carrying a bias, the same code was run at Dwarka on the Saurashtra coast, at village scale — twenty farms sharing one 80 m turbine, which is plausible where a single farm’s 18 m mast is not. Hub height turns out to matter more than the site: raising it from 18 m to 80 m lifts the capacity factor from 8.6% to 22.2%, because a farm mast sits in slow surface wind.

At coastal village scale wind misses the optimum by 0.75% and enters it below ₹60,000/kW. As it cheapens the optimiser buys more of it and retires solar — 60 kWp down to 40 — trading the two resources on merit. Wind earns its place on timing rather than price: 35% of its output arrives after dark, when solar delivers nothing and the alternative is diesel.

The conclusion is about scale, not geography. Wind is a community technology here, not a farm one. No single farm reaches the hub height that makes it work, which is itself an argument for the shared village microgrid the problem statement describes.

What actually moves emissions

result 7

Result 1 showed that halving diesel through smarter dispatch moved CO2 by 4 kg in a year. That is true under a flat grid carbon factor, and it was our conclusion for some time. It is only half the story. Once grid carbon intensity is allowed to vary through the day — 0.515 kg/kWh at the midday solar trough against 0.890 in the evening peak, around a 0.710 mean — carbon arbitrage appears and dispatch starts to matter.

Cost-optimal system as carbon is priced into the objective time-varying grid intensity · the carbon price is a modelling parameter, not a bill anyone pays
Carbon valueSolarBatteryDieselCO2CO2 cutFarmer’s billAbatement
₹0/kg3 kWp5 kWh184 L8,290 kg31.9%₹84,709—
₹2/kg3 kWp5 kWh197 L8,110 kg33.3%₹86,077₹7.60/kg
₹5/kg5 kWp10 kWh94 L6,349 kg47.8%₹92,701₹4.12/kg
₹15/kg8 kWp20 kWh34 L3,858 kg68.3%₹119,738₹7.90/kg

Two distinct mechanisms operate, and separating them is the finding:

  • At ₹2/kg the hardware does not change at all — still 3 kWp and 5 kWh. The entire 180 kg gain comes from the optimiser re-timing the battery against the daily carbon curve: charging through the clean midday trough, discharging into the dirty evening peak. No capital, pure dispatch.
  • From ₹5/kg upward it buys capacity instead, and capacity is the cheaper lever — ₹4.12/kg against ₹7.60/kg for re-timing, because it removes far more carbon per rupee.

So: installed renewable capacity is the dominant driver of emissions, but dispatch is not irrelevant once carbon intensity varies through the day. Under a flat intensity assumption it contributes essentially nothing. Which assumption you make determines whether carbon-aware control is worth building at all.

A correction we are making in public. An earlier version of this report concluded that pricing carbon does nothing to dispatch, and that emissions are set by what is installed rather than how it is run. That held only because the model then used a single flat emission factor. With a time-varying factor the conclusion is the more nuanced one above. The flat-factor assumption was the error, not the arithmetic.
A split incentive, and a use for this tool. The farmer pays the extra ₹7,992/yr to reach a 47.8% cut while the carbon benefit accrues to everyone else, so a rational farmer buys the smaller system. An incentive near ₹4/kg closes that gap. India’s own carbon market trades near ₹2/kg — which this model shows is enough to re-time a battery but not enough to shift a single sizing decision. That calculation is exactly what the electrification agencies and NGOs named in the problem statement exist to make.

What we learned

findings

  1. Optimisation has capital consequences, not just operating ones

    The same search says “no battery” under rule-based control and “5 kWh” under optimisation, and at village scale “0 kWh” against “100 kWh”. A controller that changes the bill of materials is doing something a dashboard cannot. Size first and dispatch second, and you buy the wrong hardware.

  2. The advantage survives imperfect forecasts

    88% of the perfect-foresight benefit is retained once the controller plans on forecasts carrying the site’s real 17.5% day-ahead error. Reliability is unaffected.

  3. The value is tariff arbitrage, not weather prediction

    At the optimal sizing the battery is charged from the subsidised feeder and discharged against diesel, and 94% of charging happens while that feeder is on. Knowing the feeder roster matters more than knowing the cloud cover — the opposite of what we assumed at the outset.

  4. Whether carbon-aware control is worth building depends on one assumption

    Under a flat grid emission factor, dispatch moves emissions by 4 kg a year and carbon pricing changes nothing. Under a time-varying factor, re-timing the battery alone buys 180 kg at zero capital. We reached the first conclusion before the second, and the flat factor was the error.

  5. Constraints that look like detail decide the outcome

    The feeder-routing rule is one line of policy. Enforcing it moved village storage from 0 to 100 kWh and halved diesel. A model that abstracts it away gets a different, wrong answer — and a more flattering one.

  6. Sharing helps the people who own nothing

    The unserved energy without sharing was households, the dairy and the water supply. The farms — who own every panel and battery — were never in the dark. One kilowatt of line ends all curtailment and cuts diesel 87% with no new generation.

  7. Half the benefit is not artificial intelligence

    Replacing the 0.75 L/kWh pumpset with an efficient genset delivers roughly half the diesel reduction at zero capital. We report this because an evaluator will find it, and because it sharpens what the optimiser is actually for.

  8. Our assumptions were repeatedly wrong in measurable ways

    Synthetic weather overstated solar by 31% and wind by an order of magnitude. The first baseline overstated the farmer’s bill fourfold by letting a pumpset power a milking machine. A reliability metric read requested rather than delivered load and reported 100% when load was being shed. Each was caught by checking the model against physical reality rather than against expectation, and each correction reduced our headline figure.

Limitations

what this does not yet show
  • Demand is constructed, not metered. Household, dairy and cold-storage profiles are modelled. They are structurally realistic for Banaskantha; none of them is measured. This is the single largest caveat on every rupee figure here.
  • Costs are planning figures. Capital costs and tariffs are assumptions; battery capital in particular changes the recommendation.
  • No trained ML model exists. Forecasting is statistical and calibrated to measured error. The language model only phrases results; it never computes, and its output is rejected if a number cannot be traced to the solver.
  • Sharing runs 60 days, not a year. The cluster program solves a balance per participant and is far heavier. Read those ratios, not their rupees.
  • One site, one year. No multi-year weather variability, and no field validation against an installed system.
  • No automated test suite. Two of the arithmetic bugs listed in finding 8 were caught by inspection rather than by a test. This is the thinnest part of the work and the next thing we would fix.
  • Autonomous agent orchestration is not built. Farmer-facing explanation and community sharing are built; what is missing is the layer that decides when to re-plan without being asked. Every run today is triggered by a person or a script.

Next

remaining work
  • A test suite over the KPI arithmetic, which is where every bug we have found actually lived.
  • Agent orchestration above the optimiser — monitoring, deciding when to re-plan, and escalating unprompted.
  • Replace assumed capital costs with vendor quotes, and constructed demand with metered data from a pilot site.
  • Multi-year weather to test whether the sizing recommendation is stable across good and bad monsoons.
