What the farm looks like today

baseline

A 5 HP diesel pumpset drives irrigation. It is a direct-coupled engine turning a pump shaft, so it cannot power anything else — household, dairy and cold-storage load sit on the village feeder with no backup at all. Gujarat’s Jyotigram scheme runs those two feeders separately and deliberately, and the split governs the whole problem: the agricultural feeder is three-phase and rationed to a published eight-hour block, while the village feeder is single-phase and near-continuous but too small to start the pump.

The optimiser changes what to buy

result 2

Hardware was not assumed. Every combination of solar, wind and battery was simulated across the full year, costed with capital recovered over each asset’s life, and the cheapest configuration holding reliability was selected. Running that search under each controller gives different answers — which is the central finding of the project.

Cost-optimal sizing, by controller 560-configuration sweep · identical demand, prices and reliability floor
ControllerSolarWindBatteryDiesel cutAnnual total
Rule-based2 kWp00 kWh59.0%₹86,713
Optimiser2 kWp05 kWh85.0%₹79,184

Under simple rules a battery never earns back its cost, so the optimal system has none. Under optimisation the same battery pays for itself and nearly doubles the diesel displaced. The controller is not a layer on top of the hardware — it determines which hardware is worth installing.

Why. A myopic controller reacts to the present hour. The optimiser charges the battery during the subsidised agricultural window at ₹1.50/kWh and discharges it later against diesel at ₹29.52/kWh — close to a twentyfold spread. Measured over the year, 94% of all battery charging occurs while that feeder is scheduled on.

Wind: rejected here, selected elsewhere

result 4

The problem statement names wind explicitly, so it was modelled and swept at every capacity. It was excluded from every cost-optimal configuration — and the reason turns out to be physics rather than price.

Banaskantha is inland northern Gujarat; the state’s wind resource sits on the Kutch and Saurashtra coast. A wind turbine here would be a decoration.

To confirm the engine follows the resource rather than carrying a bias, the same code was run at Dwarka on the Saurashtra coast, at village scale — twenty farms sharing one 80 m turbine, which is plausible where a single farm’s 18 m mast is not. Hub height turns out to matter more than the site: raising it from 18 m to 80 m lifts the capacity factor from 8.6% to 22.2%, because a farm mast sits in slow surface wind.

At coastal village scale wind misses the optimum by 0.75% and enters it below ₹60,000/kW. As it cheapens the optimiser buys more of it and retires solar — 60 kWp down to 40 — trading the two resources on merit. Wind earns its place on timing rather than price: 35% of its output arrives after dark, when solar delivers nothing and the alternative is diesel.

The conclusion is about scale, not geography. Wind is a community technology here, not a farm one. No single farm reaches the hub height that makes it work, which is an argument for the shared village microgrid the problem statement describes.
An incidental check. The Dwarka village works out to ₹84,348 and 185 L of diesel per farm per year. The independent single-farm Banaskantha run gave ₹84,565 and 182 L. Different site, different scale, different code path, within 0.3%.

Emissions come from capacity, not control

result 5

Carbon was priced into both the dispatch objective and the sizing objective. Dispatch barely responds: grid power is 0.71 kg CO2/kWh and diesel 0.81, so once battery round-trip losses are counted the two are within a few percent and the controller has no cleaner option to switch to. Even at ₹50/kg, emissions move under 1%. Capacity is the lever.

Expressed as marginal abatement cost, moving from a 35% to a 49% emissions cut costs ₹3.10 per kg CO2 avoided — below most estimates of the social cost of carbon. Pushing on to 69% costs ₹8.22/kg.

A split incentive, and a use for this tool. The farmer pays the extra ₹5,200/yr while the carbon benefit accrues to everyone else, so a rational farmer buys the smaller system. An incentive of ₹3–5/kg closes the gap and buys 14 further points of emissions reduction at below social cost. India’s own carbon market trades near ₹2/kg — which this model shows is too weak to shift a single sizing decision. That calculation is exactly the decision the electrification agencies and NGOs named in the problem statement exist to make.

What we learned

findings

  1. Optimisation has capital consequences, not just operating ones

    The same search says “no battery” under rule-based control and “5 kWh” under optimisation. A controller that changes the bill of materials is doing something a dashboard cannot.

  2. The advantage survives imperfect forecasts

    88% of the perfect-foresight benefit is retained once the controller plans on forecasts carrying the site’s real 17.5% day-ahead error. Reliability is unaffected.

  3. The value is tariff arbitrage, not weather prediction

    At the optimal sizing the battery is charged from the subsidised feeder and discharged against diesel. Knowing the feeder roster matters more than knowing the cloud cover — the opposite of what we assumed at the outset.

  4. Emissions are decided at purchase, not at runtime

    No dispatch policy meaningfully changes CO2, because the alternatives are within a few percent of each other. Only installed renewable capacity moves it.

  5. Half the benefit is not artificial intelligence

    Replacing the 0.75 L/kWh pumpset with an efficient genset delivers roughly half the diesel reduction at zero capital. We report this because an evaluator will find it, and because it sharpens what the optimiser is actually for.

  6. Assumptions were repeatedly wrong in measurable ways

    Our synthetic weather overstated solar by 31% and wind by an order of magnitude; our first baseline overstated the farmer’s bill fourfold by letting a pumpset power a milking machine. Each was caught by checking the model against physical reality rather than against expectation.

Limitations

what this does not yet show
  • Demand is synthetic. Household, dairy and cold-storage profiles are constructed, not metered, and cold storage alone drives roughly 40% of demand. Irrigation is grounded in surveyed pump hours.
  • Costs are planning figures. Capital costs and tariffs are assumptions; battery capital in particular changes the recommendation.
  • One farm, one year. Results are for a single representative site over calendar 2025, with no multi-year weather variability.
  • No field validation. Nothing here has been measured against an installed system.
  • The agent layer is not built. Phases 5–7 remain ahead; all present results come from the deterministic core.

Next

remaining work
  • Agent layer over the optimiser for monitoring, replanning and plain-language explanation to the farmer.
  • Operator dashboard and farmer-facing recommendations, driven by the existing engine.
  • Community extension — several farms sharing generation and storage, which the sizing engine already supports in principle.
  • Replace assumed capital costs with vendor quotes, and synthetic demand with metered data from a pilot site.
